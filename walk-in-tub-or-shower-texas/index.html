Choosing well

Walk-in tub or accessible shower: which fits your Texas home?

The right answer comes from how the person moves and bathes, not from a brochure. Use the questions below to narrow it down before you call.

Four options, briefly

  • Walk-in tub: a watertight door, a low step, a built-in seat and deep water. You sit while it fills and empties.
  • Transfer tub: a walk-in tub with a wide outward door and a seat placed so a person can slide over from a wheelchair or bench.
  • Walk-in shower: a low curb or beveled entry, a fold-down or freestanding seat, and a handheld shower.
  • Roll-in shower: an entry designed for wheels to cross, often with a collapsible water dam or a trench drain at the opening.

Five questions that usually decide it

1. Can the person lift a foot a few inches, safely, every day?

If not, skip the walk-in tub and look at a roll-in shower or a transfer tub.

2. Does a warm soak matter to them?

Some people with stiff joints treasure a long soak, and a shower will feel like a loss. Others never bathe and simply want to rinse safely. Be honest about which one you are planning for.

3. Will someone help with bathing?

Caregivers need room to stand and reach. A roll-in shower or an outward-opening tub door gives that room. An inward door in a narrow bath does not.

4. How long will they live in this home?

If the plan is ten more years and mobility is likely to decline, a roll-in shower may be the one remodel that keeps working. If the need is shorter-term or the person is still fairly mobile, a walk-in tub can be ideal now.

5. What does the house allow?

Alcove size, slab versus pier-and-beam, water heater capacity and the path into the bathroom all matter. Our Texas homes guide covers these.

Texas notes

  • Heat: in a long Texas summer, a warm soak may be less appealing than a quick cool shower. In winter, heated seats and quick drains make walk-in tubs comfortable.
  • Hard water in many parts of the state leaves scale on glass doors and jets. Ask about cleaning and, if needed, a softener.
  • Slab homes: a shower drain sits in a different place than a tub drain, so converting a tub to a shower can mean cutting concrete. A trench drain or a pan with a matching drain location can help.

When to bring in a therapist

An occupational therapist can watch the person move in their own bathroom and recommend the safest setup. Ask the doctor whether a referral makes sense, and ask your plan what it covers. Bring their notes to the quote conversation and we will work from them.

Two-bathroom households

In a home with two baths, many Texas families convert one and leave the other alone. A spouse who still enjoys a standard tub keeps it, and the person who needs safer access gets a walk-in tub or roll-in shower in the bathroom closest to their bedroom. If there is only one full bath, think hard about everyone who uses it before choosing.

Thinking about resale

Buyers vary. A well-finished roll-in shower can appeal to a broad range of people; a walk-in tub appeals strongly to some. If resale matters, a good-looking finish and solid workmanship count more than the specific fixture.

Common questions

Is a roll-in shower better than a walk-in tub for a wheelchair user?

Usually, because it has no step. A transfer tub with a wide outward door is an option for people who can slide from a chair to a seat.

Which is easier for a caregiver?

A roll-in shower usually gives the most room to help. An outward-opening walk-in tub door also helps.

Can I convert a tub to a shower on a slab foundation?

Yes, though the drain may need to move through the concrete. A pan with a matching drain location or a trench drain can simplify it.
